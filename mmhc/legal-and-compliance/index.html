Manhattan Mental Health Counseling · Content Writer Brief

Legal & Compliance

Manhattan Mental Health Counseling LLC is a New York State, 100% online psychotherapy practice founded in 2014, led by clinical director Natalie Buchwald, LMHC-D. Everything below reflects the compliance patterns MMHC actually uses in its published blogs. When a judgment call arises, match the most cautious example in their live content rather than the most aggressive.

Safety comes first, on every post

Any article that touches distress, anxiety, trauma, depression, self-harm, eating, substances, or simply "not coping" must carry a crisis line near the end of the body, never buried in the footer. On educational posts MMHC uses language along the lines of: if you are thinking about harming yourself or your distress feels unmanageable, do not rely on this exercise alone; in the United States, call or text 988 for immediate crisis support, and if you are in immediate danger, call 911. On coverage and service pages it phrases the same idea as a short "In crisis?" note pointing to 988 for free, confidential support and 911 or the nearest emergency room for immediate danger. Either phrasing is fine; the presence of it is not optional.

Attribution and review

Each editorial post is published under a named, credentialed clinician (for example, Natalie Buchwald, LMHC-D), linked to their author page, and carries a separate "Reviewed by" line naming a second person, such as Steven Buchwald or Natalie Buchwald herself. Every post shows a published date and a "Last updated" date. Evergreen coverage and cost pages go further and stamp a review cadence, noting when the page was last updated and when it is next scheduled for review, roughly quarterly on insurance pages. Placeholder bylines must never survive to publication. One recent post still carried a bracketed "By [Therapist Name], [LMHC/…]" stand-in, and that kind of placeholder has to be filled with a real, credentialed name before the piece goes live.

Say what MMHC is, accurately

The clinicians are Licensed Mental Health Counselors and limited-permit holders, not psychiatrists, and they provide psychotherapy and counseling rather than medical or prescribing services. Content must never imply that MMHC prescribes medication, and it must never tell a reader to start, stop, or change any medication or dose. When medication comes up, describe it neutrally, acknowledge the therapy-versus-medication trade-offs without steering anyone off something a prescriber has given them, and route medication questions to a psychiatrist or prescriber. The practice serves New York State residents only, delivered by secure, HIPAA-compliant video, with audio-only sessions available where video is not workable. Do not claim national service. Even where a payer like Original Medicare technically works nationwide, MMHC treats clients who are physically located in New York at the time of the session, and the writing needs to preserve that distinction rather than promise cross-state care.

Discipline around claims

This is where mental-health blogs get a practice into trouble, and MMHC's own content is careful, so the writing must be too. Never guarantee an individual's coverage or price. Follow the practice's habit of hedging with "most beneficiaries," "typically," "about," and "your exact cost depends on your plan," always paired with the reassurance that the practice verifies each person's exact cost before the first session. Any specific figure, a deductible, a copay range, a "no session limits" statement, has to be tied to a dated, authoritative source such as CMS, HHS, KFF, medicare.gov, the New York Department of Financial Services, or New York State of Health, with the source named near the figure and cited fully at the end. Keep plan rules distinct from legal mandates; MMHC will write, for instance, that something is Medicare's own coverage rule and not a state mandate, and that precision matters. Give readers ways to verify coverage themselves, pointing them to 1-800-MEDICARE, medicare.gov, or New York's HIICAP counseling program, rather than funneling every question into "call us," which is part of what makes the content read as trustworthy. Define jargon in plain language the first time it appears, the way MMHC explains Medigap, dual eligibility, coinsurance, and "medically necessary" inline.

On the clinical side, avoid cures, guarantees, and promised timelines. Use "can help," "may," "often," "suggests," and "associated with." Distinguish self-help from treatment: whenever a post offers a technique, it should also carry a boundary, in the spirit of MMHC's "what this is not" passages, making clear that the exercise is not a substitute for assessment or care and that genuine danger calls for professional help. Label any coined phrase or metaphor as non-clinical, exactly as MMHC notes that a term like "alarm versus forecast" is not a clinical term but a useful way to capture a distinction therapists help clients make. Cite peer-reviewed research for any mechanism or efficacy claim, and never diagnose the reader, frame symptoms as signs that may indicate something and point toward professional assessment rather than telling someone they have a disorder.

Sourcing

Research-heavy and coverage posts carry inline hyperlinks on the specific claim plus a references list at the end, written in a clean journal or author-date style. Prefer primary and authoritative sources: peer-reviewed journals and PubMed, government sites like CMS, HHS, and NIMH, major professional bodies such as the APA and AMA, and official state agencies. Avoid citing competitor blogs or content mills. Anything time-sensitive, costs, deductibles, telehealth policy, plan availability, should cite the current, dated version and name the year, the way MMHC labels its figures as 2026. Internal links to MMHC's own service, insurance, and related blog pages are used generously and are good practice, but they never substitute for an external authority on a factual claim.

Sensitive topics

Several recent posts sit in higher-risk territory, and each has its own rules. For eating disorders and body image, never include numbers that could function as targets, no calories, weights, BMIs, "goal" figures, or restriction and purging how-tos, and frame everything around function, distress, and control rather than appearance; if a helpline is named, use the National Alliance for Eating Disorders rather than NEDA. For self-harm and suicide, never describe methods or means even to warn against them, lead with the crisis line and destigmatizing language, and avoid reflective writing that amplifies hopelessness. For trauma and PTSD, avoid graphic detail and follow MMHC's framing that trauma responses are not failures of character, willpower, or faith, emphasizing that a careful assessment and the right therapist matter more than a reader self-selecting a treatment label. For substances and addiction, including any plant-medicine or psychedelic material, keep the writing educational and destigmatizing and confined to the integration and therapy level, never a how-to. The audience is adults throughout; where child, ADHD, or parenting topics appear, address the caregiving adult and keep the treatment clinical.

Testimonials, proof points, and marketing

Client testimonials are used, but they stay verbatim and attributable, drawn from real reviews with first-name or Google-review attribution, and are never fabricated or embellished. State the rating MMHC currently publishes and keep it consistent with the live site; newer pages show a 4.0 Google rating alongside "5,000+ New Yorkers served since 2014," so avoid inventing star ratings or carrying forward an older, higher figure. Only publish verified, citable proof points, founded in 2014, more than 5,000 clients served, 91-plus therapists, fully online, all clinicians Medicare-enrolled with active NPIs, and leave out any number that has not been verified. When profiling clinicians, show their real credentials and license numbers, as MMHC does, and never attribute a credential a clinician does not hold. Comparisons to named competitors such as Talkspace, BetterHelp, Alma, and Headway should stay factual and defensible, resting on real differences like New-York-specific expertise, human matching, and licensed clinicians versus self-serve tools, rather than unsupported superiority claims.

Privacy

Describe sessions as HIPAA-compliant and secure, and do not make promises about the practice's systems beyond what MMHC itself states, such as its use of Theranest. Never publish or invent client-identifying details or protected health information; illustrative "you" scenarios are fine, but real client stories are not unless they already exist as published, attributed testimonials. Link the site's Privacy Notice and Emergency Resources pages where relevant.
