Voice & Tone

The voice in a sentence

MMHC sounds like a compassionate, intelligent, empowering New York therapist who validates what you are feeling, explains the science plainly, and hands you something concrete to do about it. It never talks down, never hypes, and never shames. It treats the reader as a capable adult who is struggling with something real.

Point of view

MMHC deliberately moves between two points of view. Second person, "you", is the default for the reader's inner world, and it is used to describe symptoms, feelings, and steps in an immersive, validating way, as in the opening line of a recent post: your chest tightens before a difficult conversation, and your mind hands down a verdict that this will go badly. First person plural, "we" and "our", is reserved for the practice and what it does, offers, and verifies, as in "we verify your exact cost before your first session" or "our ADHD behavior therapists work with you." Keep the two separate: "you" carries the reader's experience, "we" carries the practice's help. Avoid slipping into a detached, third-person clinical register.

How it should feel

The tone is compassionate, so it validates the struggle before it does anything else, without tipping into pity or catastrophizing. It is empowering, telling the reader that a skill is learnable or that they can work with their brain rather than against it, while steering clear of toxic positivity or "just think positive." It is clinically credible, citing research and using the correct terms before translating them, but it never dumps jargon or leaves an acronym unexplained. It is actively destigmatizing, describing struggles as not failures of character, willpower, or faith rather than moralizing about weakness. And it is calm and grounded, steady and reassuring and adult-to-adult, never alarmist, salesy, or breathless. Warm but not casual: friendly and plain-spoken, without slang, memes, jokey filler, or a spray of exclamation points.

How a post is built

Recent posts follow a recognizable arc. They open with a short, immersive, validating moment that drops the reader into a felt experience or names a struggle directly, often in the present tense and often sensory, life with ADHD can feel like following a GPS that keeps rerouting. That opening is followed by a crisp reframe or thesis, frequently a two-part statement that turns the problem hopeful, such as the fear is real, but the forecast may not be. From there the post explains the mechanism, what is actually happening in the cycle, the brain, or the science, in plain language backed by linked sources, and then gives the reader a concrete tool or framework, whether that is a set of numbered steps, a named technique, a "try this," a checklist in prose, or an honest comparison. It scopes and caveats what it has offered, marking what the technique is not and when to seek real help, with the crisis line here. It closes with a soft, topic-specific call to action that bridges the article's theme to MMHC's help rather than selling hard, and, on research or coverage posts, a references section. Section headers lean on questions the reader is actually asking, like "why isn't therapy working?" or "what does Medicare therapy cost in New York?"

Sentences and rhythm

Paragraphs are short, usually one to four sentences, with frequent single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis. The rhythm varies: longer explanatory sentences interleaved with short punches, and at times a three-word landing like "that skill is learnable." Plain words come first and the clinical term second, describe noticing a thought as a mental event rather than a fact, then name it as decentering. Keep it concrete over abstract, using everyday examples such as a delayed reply becoming rejection or rereading an email ten times, and use gentle imperatives in the how-to passages: send the message, attend the meeting, book the appointment.

Formatting, used lightly

MMHC's posts are scannable, but the scaffolding stays in service of the reading. A single bolded line often carries the key takeaway of a section. Italics mark the reader's inner thoughts and self-talk, like the quiet "why can't I just focus like everyone else?" Numbered steps suit techniques and short lists suit signs, benefits, or options, always written as full enough phrases to stand on their own. Headers are phrased as the reader's own questions, and service and insurance posts usually close with a short run of frequently asked questions. The rule of thumb is restraint: no walls of bold, and no stacked headers with nothing between them.

Vocabulary and signature moves

The natural vocabulary includes empower, resilience, clarity, grounded, nervous system, evidence-based, tools and strategies, sustainable, compassion, self-awareness, holistic, and mind-body, along with the recurring reassurances that you are not alone and that with the right support things can change. A few moves are distinctive to the brand. New York imagery grounds the reader in place, the subway, the commute, the city fueled by speed, Manhattan and Brooklyn and Queens. Metaphor and analogy make clinical ideas stick, from a GPS that keeps rerouting to a smoke alarm to quicksand, with any coined phrase flagged as non-clinical. Two-part reframes recur, as in "ADHD doesn't define you, you define it." And occasionally a piece reaches for genuine intellectual or cultural depth, using a philosophical or literary reference respectfully to illuminate an idea rather than to show off, the way one recent anxiety post drew on a Hasidic reading of fear; that move works only when it earns its place, so use it sparingly.

What to avoid is the mirror image of all this: guarantees and absolutes like "cure," "always," or "instantly"; fear-mongering; marketing hype; clichéd wellness fluff such as "just breathe" or "good vibes"; stigmatizing words like "crazy" or "broken," except where a piece is explicitly reclaiming and reframing them; and any jargon left unexplained.

The call to action

Calls to action stay soft and tied to the theme of the piece, bridging from the topic to help, if fear is shrinking your life, structured therapy may help. MMHC weaves in familiar, low-pressure details: a ten-minute call to verify insurance, a roster of 91-plus therapists, an average copay range, and a Therapy Coordinator who can match you, ending on "Start Therapy Now." Lead with the reader's benefit, relief, a good match, low cost, starting this week, rather than urgency, and keep the practice's warmth, as in a simple "we'd love to hear from you."

A couple of small conventions

Spell out small numbers in prose where it reads naturally and use figures for statistics, ages, costs, steps, and data. Em dashes are used freely for asides and rhythm. Spelling is American. Headers are mostly sentence case and phrased as questions where possible. The best final check is to read the draft aloud: if it sounds like an advertisement or a lecture, revise, because it should sound like a wise, warm clinician who respects the reader.

The quick test

A line that sounds like MMHC might be: you don't need to silence the alarm, you need to hear it without automatically believing its forecast, and that skill is learnable, with a therapist to help you practice it. A line that does not would be something like: anxiety ruining your life? Our amazing therapists will cure your worry fast, guaranteed, sign up now. The first validates, reframes, stays honest about outcomes, and offers help calmly. The second hypes, promises, and pressures. Always write the first.
